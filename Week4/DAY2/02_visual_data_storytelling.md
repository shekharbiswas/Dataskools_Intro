# 📅 Visual Data Storytelling

Today is about transforming data into a visual story that others can quickly understand — and even feel something about. 

A well-crafted chart or slide can do more than just show numbers; it can reveal patterns, spark curiosity, and help people make decisions. 

Whether you're presenting to a class, a client, or just building your own skills, learning how to communicate insights visually is a core part of becoming a data-literate thinker.


## 🎨 Task: Create a Visual Slide or Story

Use [**Canva**](https://www.canva.com/) and Google Slides to create one impactful slide or visual.




### 🎯 Your Visual Must Include:

To tell a strong data story, your visual should combine **clarity**, **insight**, and **design**. Include the following elements:

- **📊 A Chart or Graphic**  
  Choose the right type of visual for your data:
  - **Bar chart** for comparing categories (e.g., sales by flavor)
  - **Pie chart** for showing proportions (e.g., share of total revenue)
  - **Line chart** for trends over time (e.g., cups sold per day)
  - **Scatter plot** for relationships (e.g., price vs cups sold)
  - **Infographic or mixed layout** if you want to combine data, text, and icons

  Make sure the chart:
  - Has a clear title
  - Uses proper labels (axis names, categories, units)
  - Isn’t cluttered — avoid adding too many variables in one graph

## 📊 Charts in Google Sheets

Google Sheets is a powerful tool for turning raw data into visual insights — no coding required. Here’s a guide to the chart types you can create and when to use them.

<br>

### ✅ Bar Chart – Compare Categories

**Use when:** You want to compare values across categories (e.g., cups sold by flavor).

**Example use case:**  
"Which lemonade flavor sells the most?"

**How to create:**
1. Select your data (e.g., Flavor and Cups Sold)
2. Click `Insert > Chart`
3. In the Chart Editor, choose **Column chart** or **Bar chart**

**Best practices:**
- Keep category names short and clear
- Sort data for better readability (e.g., descending order)

<br>

### ✅ Pie Chart – Show Proportions

**Use when:** You want to show how a whole is divided into parts (e.g., revenue share by flavor).

**Example use case:**  
"What percentage of total sales comes from each flavor?"

**How to create:**
1. Select two columns: Category (e.g., Flavor) and Value (e.g., Total Revenue)
2. Click `Insert > Chart`
3. Choose **Pie chart**

**Best practices:**
- Limit to 4–6 categories to avoid clutter
- Use data labels to show percentages

<br>

### ✅ Line Chart – Show Trends Over Time

**Use when:** You want to visualize how something changes over time (e.g., daily sales).

**Example use case:**  
"How do cups sold change each day?"

**How to create:**
1. Select Date and Value columns (e.g., Date and Cups Sold)
2. Click `Insert > Chart`
3. Choose **Line chart**

**Best practices:**
- Make sure dates are formatted correctly
- Sort data chronologically

<br>

### ✅ Scatter Plot – Explore Relationships

**Use when:** You want to see if two numeric variables are related (e.g., price vs cups sold).

**Example use case:**  
"Does lower price lead to more sales?"

**How to create:**
1. Select your X and Y value columns (e.g., Price and Cups Sold)
2. Click `Insert > Chart`
3. Choose **Scatter chart**

**Best practices:**
- Use trendline (Customize > Series > Trendline) to highlight patterns
- Remove outliers that may skew results

<br>

### ⚠️ Infographic or Mixed Layout

**Use when:** You want to combine text, visuals, and data to tell a story.

**How to create:**
- While Google Sheets doesn't have built-in infographic tools, you can:
  - Use **bold headers**, cell background colors, and emojis
  - Insert **images, drawings, or shapes** via `Insert > Drawing` or `Insert > Image`
  - Arrange charts and KPIs on a dedicated **dashboard sheet**
  - Add custom labels and takeaways in adjacent cells

**Better alternative:**  
For advanced design and layout, use **Canva**, **Google Slides**, or **PowerPoint** and embed your Google Sheets charts.

<br>

### 💡 Pro Tip

To present your visuals:
- Copy any chart and paste it into a **Google Slides** presentation
- This gives you more layout control and makes your story more engaging

<br>

- **💬 A Brief Explanation (2–3 Sentences)**  
  Your visual needs context. Add a short, written takeaway that explains:
  - What the chart shows
  - Why it’s interesting
  - What insight a viewer should remember

  Example:  
  > “This chart compares sales across three lemonade flavors. Lemon consistently outperformed the others, especially on weekends. This suggests customer preference may be linked to refreshing taste during warmer days.”

- **🧼 Clean Layout and Readable Text**  
  Presentation matters. A great chart can lose its impact if it’s messy or hard to read.

  Design tips:
  - Use high contrast colors (avoid neon or overly light shades)
  - Make text at least 14pt size for headings, 10–12pt for labels
  - Align elements properly (e.g., center titles, avoid overlapping text)
  - Leave white space — don’t cram too much into one slide
  - Consider adding icons or light visuals to guide the eye

## 🧠 Why This Matters

Visuals are the most powerful tool in a data analyst’s toolkit. They help people:
- Grasp complex patterns instantly
- Connect emotionally with numbers
- Make smarter decisions quickly

A great visual doesn’t need a long explanation — it *shows* the story clearly.
